# Game Design Principles Essay
The three game design principles I will be analyzing are introducing elements in isolation, risk vs reward, and deliberately simplifying gameplay.


Introducing elements in isolation means exposing the player to new gameplay mechanics, systems, enemies, concepts or tools one at time so they fully understand it before building upon that foundation with more elements. This works because the player can focus cognitively on learning one thing, or a group of relating things without being overwhelmed. It also links a clear cause and effect relationship between the element being introduced and the consequences of engaging with it. The main con would be potentially taking a while to build complete experience for the game.


A strong example is the original Super Mario Bros. In a 2015 interview done by Eurogamer with game director Shigeru Miyamoto, he explains the level design of 1-1 and how it gradually introduces elements to the player in succession. He details having a goomba walk at the player and nothing else, forcing them to learn how to at the very least jump and move from left to right. After this, blocks are placed above, and the player is incentivized to use the jump they just learned to hit them, rewarding them with coins at first, and then a power-up. This is supposed to introduce the player to each element in order of importance. In quick succession, the player is introduced to the essential gameplay mechanics one by one and is now ready to play the game. 


Risk vs reward revolves around players having to make a meaningful choice that considers the possible benefits compared to the consequences of failing. Typically, the more benefits or rewards are offered, the worse the consequence is or more skill is required. 


Super Smash Bros showcases this well. Faster moves are usually considered safe, being either hard to react to, land consistently, or do not require a lot of commitment. The payoff usually is that they do not do much damage. Many characters have moves that aren’t safe. They have cons like being easily telegraphed, hard to land, and leaving the player in a lot of end lag. The potential consequence of the decision to use one of these moves is the opponent capitalizing and damaging you instead. The reward is landing a lot of damage or taking a stock off your opponent.  This game mechanic encourages the player to get better at the game so that they feel more confident making and executing risky decisions, reaping the benefits. It also is forgiving to newer players by giving them the choice to back out. The main con of this is new players potentially feeling left out by not being able to do skilled tasks.


Deliberate simplification is a principle where building upon previously established gameplay mechanics, level design, etc. is negated to make the experience streamlined. This can lower the level of skill needed to play the game as well as make things consistent but comes at the cost of shallow engagement.


A game that encapsulates this is Sonic Forces. Compared to previous 3D Sonic entries, this game stripped away many gameplay mechanics and heavily simplified level design, sterilizing the overall experience. In a gameplay showcase with NHK World Japan, game designer Shun Nakamura explained that you can simply hold a button to attack and “simply enjoy dashing like a maniac” through the stages. The cost of this was short, on rails level design with minimal input or punishment. 


In terms of how I might use these principles in the game I will develop for this class, introducing elements in isolation will be essential for anybody playing that isn’t me. My game will be inspired by 2D action platformers like Megaman X, so in a tutorial level, I want to take an approach of introducing movement in a forgiving environment, then shooting enemies with minimal difficulty, and then killing them with a blade. If the player suffers damage during this encounter, they will be presented with a health item to introduce the concept of healing as well. This foundation of movement and engaging with enemies will be built on as the game goes on to make the experience easy to comprehend and more engaging.


The same mindset goes for incorporating risk vs reward. A mechanic I would like to implement is a parry that encourages players to get up close with enemies more instead of just spamming a projectile. Before an attack, an enemy will flash briefly to indicate it can be parried, if a player parries this successfully, it will leave the enemy in a compromised state where the player can either deal a lot of damage or kill it immediately (depending on the type of enemy.) The risk would be going in to perform the action and potentially taking damage upon failure. The reward would be a parry bonus and being able to advance quicker. As a player gets more experienced with the game, they should have no problem doing this and reaping the benefits.


Deliberate simplicity is something I want to avoid when designing my game. I want to make sure my game builds upon its base gameplay loop of running, shooting and jumping by having engaging level design that gets harder as the game goes on, different enemies that force the player to engage differently, and unlockable items that complement the base gameplay loop.
